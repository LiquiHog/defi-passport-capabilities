# Business revenue

Crypto coming in, ordinary bills going out, and no surprises in between.

A business taking payment in crypto has a timing problem. Revenue arrives in volatile assets at unpredictable times; suppliers, salaries and tax obligations are denominated in something stable and due on fixed dates. The gap between those is where businesses get hurt.

## The setup

A business taking around 40,000 USDC-equivalent monthly, with 30,000 in fixed costs:

| Purpose | Amount | State |
|---|---|---|
| Operating account — 3 months | 90,000 USDC | **Locked**, drawn down monthly |
| Supplier payments | Funded per cycle | [Recurring payments](../commerce/recurring-payments.md) |
| Surplus investment | 25,000 | [Rebalancing](../../strategies/portfolio/rebalancing.md), conservative |
| Buffer | 15,000 | Available |

The operating account holds stable value, not whatever was received. Revenue that arrives as a volatile asset gets converted on the way in, not left to drift until the bill is due.

## Handling incoming revenue

The order that avoids trouble:

1. **Top up operations first.** Bring the account back to three months before anything else.
2. **Fund the buffer.** Cover known irregular costs.
3. **Invest what's genuinely spare.** Only what's left.

Businesses that invert this — investing first and topping up with what's left — discover the problem during a bad month, which is also when the investment is down.

## Adapting it

Convert on receipt rather than holding volatile assets you didn't choose to hold. A payment received in a token is a token you now own on purpose or by accident.

Automate supplier payments where amounts are fixed and predictable.

Put the surplus in [lending](../../strategies/lending/idle-capital.md) rather than a trading strategy if you'd rather not think about it.

## What to watch for

**Don't hold your tax obligation in a volatile asset.** Money owed in fiat should be held in something stable from the moment you know you owe it.

**Revenue isn't smooth.** A three-month operating account assumes bad months happen. Size it from your worst quarter, not your average one.

**Keep business and speculation separate.** The surplus allocation should be capital the business could lose without a problem. If it can't, it isn't surplus.
