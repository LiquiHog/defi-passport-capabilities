# Active versus idle capital

Working out how much you can afford to have tied up — the question everything else depends on.

Most treasuries hold more cash than they need. Putting that to work is obvious; the hard part is being honest about which part is genuinely spare, because getting it wrong is only discovered at the worst possible time.

## Working it out

In this order:

1. **Real monthly costs.** What you actually spend, not the budget.
2. **How many months** you'd want if income stopped entirely. Three is a floor, six is comfortable.
3. **Large dated commitments.** An audit, a conference, a contract renewal, a tax bill.
4. **What's left** is a candidate for deployment.

A treasury of 10,000 spending 2,000 a month with a 3,000 audit due in four months has 6,000 of runway plus 3,000 committed — so 1,000 is genuinely spare, not 4,000.

## The three tiers

| Tier | Available | Holds |
|---|---|---|
| **Immediate** | Right now | Operating account, buffer |
| **Short notice** | Days | [Lending](../../strategies/lending/idle-capital.md), liquid allocations |
| **Committed** | Weeks or on its own terms | Trading inventory, longer positions |

Match each obligation to a tier it can actually be paid from. Anything due within a month belongs in the first.

## Adapting it

Ladder the middle tier so capital becomes available in stages rather than all at once.

Automate the top-up so strategy profits refill the operating account before anything else. See [profit allocation](profit-management.md).

Revisit quarterly. Costs and obligations both move.

## What to watch for

**Total value isn't available cash.** A treasury worth 10,000 with 4,000 deployed cannot spend 10,000 tomorrow. Plans that confuse these break under stress.

**Withdrawals are slowest when you need them most.** Market stress is exactly when lending markets are most utilized and everyone wants out at once.

**Confirm before committing.** Withdraw, watch it land, then fund the next thing. A multi-step plan funded from expected proceeds fails quietly.

**Optimism compounds.** Each slightly generous assumption — costs a bit low, runway a bit short, a commitment forgotten — stacks into a treasury with far less slack than you thought.
